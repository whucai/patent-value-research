\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}
Universities with the same number of patents can produce them through
different knowledge processes: one draws on a narrow set of external sources
and refines an established core; the other connects technical elements that
rarely appear together and is later diffused across organizations outside its
own field. Patent counts, grants, and citation frequency record the output of
both institutions but not the difference between them
\citep{griliches1990,hall2005,nagaoka2010}. Evaluation of university patent
technological innovation capability has therefore long relied on
result-oriented indicators that reflect the scale or external impact of
output, while leaving the internal capabilities that operate during the
innovation process---knowledge sourcing, recombination, and
diffusion---largely unobserved. As university patent output keeps growing,
identifying this capability and its formation mechanisms from the
knowledge-association structures carried by patents has become a central
problem in innovation evaluation, and one that conventional approaches, which
under-represent the formation process, leave unresolved.

Information science already treats documents as relational objects.
Bibliographic coupling and co-citation identify intellectual connections
\citep{kessler1963,small1973,price1965}; knowledge maps represent domains and
their change over time \citep{borner2003,chen2006,vaneck2010}; patent overlays
extend these methods to technology
\citep{rafols2010,kay2014,leydesdorff2014,yan2017}. What is missing is a way to
move from a map, which shows where an institution is located at one moment, to a
capability measure that shows how its knowledge relations are sourced,
combined, explored, diffused, and reorganized---and whether observed change
exceeds sampling and measurement noise.

This paper proposes such a measurement framework for Chinese university
patents. The central idea is simple. Patent relations of different
kinds---shared classifications, textual proximity, and citations---express
different knowledge relationships and become observable at different times.
Fusing them into a multi-dimensional knowledge-association structure, and
dating each one, makes it possible to characterize a university's patent
technological innovation capability along five dimensions: \emph{knowledge
sourcing} (the reach and diversity of external knowledge a university builds
on), \emph{knowledge recombination} (the rarity of the technical-element
combinations it forms), \emph{technological exploration} (the novelty of the
directions it enters relative to its own past), \emph{knowledge diffusion}
(the breadth and uptake of its knowledge among later users), and
\emph{dynamic transformation} (sustained reorganization of its technological
composition across periods). The first four are observable when an invention
is published or shortly after; the fifth is assessed independently across
periods against a within-university null, so that future structural change is
not used to construct a state that is then said to explain that change. The
two research questions are:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{RQ\arabic*.}]
\item How can patent knowledge-association structures be used to measure
different dimensions of university patent technological innovation
capability?
\item To what extent can a knowledge-association-based framework reveal
university patent technological innovation capability beyond conventional
output-oriented indicators?
\end{enumerate}

Using a longitudinal corpus of invention patents from 124 Chinese
Double-First-Class universities (1990--2019), this paper demonstrates the
empirical validity of the framework. The core contribution is a traceable
link between knowledge organization and capability measurement: every
profile value and pattern statement can be followed back to dated patent
records and the specific relations that support it. The paper specifies the
framework, the measures, and the reporting design, while explicitly
identifying what a university-only patent and citation corpus can and cannot
support.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}
Patents are selective records of knowledge production. Not all inventions are
patented, quality varies, and legal and organizational strategies shape the
record \citep{griliches1990,nagaoka2010}. Citations require particular care:
examiner-added references weaken their reading as inventor learning
\citep{alcacer2006}, and knowledge-flow estimates depend on technological
controls and comparison groups \citep{jaffe1993,thompson2005}. Patents are
therefore treated here as documented technical objects with dated relations,
not as complete histories of invention.

Innovation-capability evaluation has nonetheless relied heavily on these same
output indicators. Patent counts, grant counts, and citation frequency summarize
the scale or external impact of inventive output, but universities producing
the same volume can differ in how they source, combine, and diffuse technical
knowledge \citep{griliches1990,hall2005}. Aggregating across heterogeneous
knowledge processes into a single performance score obscures these
differences and offers little leverage on how innovation advantages form. The
framework here keeps the underlying relations visible so that capability is
read from structure rather than summarized by count.

Three relation types are used in patent mapping. Classification systems
organize technical categories \citep{leydesdorff2008}; text exposes
similarities that shared codes miss \citep{whalen2020,kelly2021,sarica2020};
citations record sourcing and later use. Overlay maps place organizations in a
common knowledge space \citep{rafols2010,kay2014}, and relatedness research
links positions in that space to entry and exit \citep{kogler2013,rigby2015}.
Layout movement, however, can arise from a changed sample or algorithm rather
than from changed knowledge. This study keeps visual coordinates fixed and
measures change in the underlying distributions.

The process vocabulary comes from innovation research. Absorptive capacity
concerns the recognition and use of external knowledge \citep{cohen1990};
exploration and exploitation \citep{march1991} and search depth and scope
\citep{katila2002} describe learning orientations; combinatorial and
atypical-combination studies motivate measuring unusual connections separately
from their later impact \citep{fleming2001,uzzi2013,youn2015,verhoeven2016,wang2017}.
Emergence is more than novelty: its attributes include growth, coherence,
impact, and uncertainty \citep{rotolo2015}, of which scientometric methods
operationalize a subset \citep{small2014}. First citation and later accumulation
carry different temporal information \citep{huang2019}. Multilayer network
research shows why heterogeneous relations should not be summed into one
adjacency matrix \citep{kivela2014,boccaletti2014}. The present framework draws
on these sources but reframes two constructs for a capability-structure
reading: the novelty--growth signal is read as \emph{technological exploration}
of new directions rather than field emergence, and the breadth and uptake of
later citations is read as \emph{knowledge diffusion} rather than bounded
reuse. Throughout, patent references index documented sourcing, not
capability; rare code pairs index unusual combination, not usefulness; later
citing records index bounded diffusion, not learning.

\section{Framework: relations, time, and transformation}\label{sec:framework}
University patent technological innovation capability is defined here as the
capacity to source, combine, explore, diffuse, and reorganize technical
knowledge, as observed in the relations carried by a university's patents.
Patents are the observable objects; universities are the contexts in which they
accumulate and connect. No patent or institution is assigned an intrinsic
developmental type.

Figure~\ref{fig:framework} shows the design. Four concurrent dimensions
describe a university-period profile,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}_{ut}=(A_{ut},R_{ut},E_{ut},D_{ut})^{\mathsf T},
\label{eq:profile}
\end{equation}
where $A$ (knowledge sourcing) and $R$ (recombination) are observable when
inventions are published, $E$ (technological exploration) uses the current
cohort against its own historical reference, and $D$ (knowledge diffusion)
requires a fixed follow-up horizon. The fifth dimension, dynamic
transformation $T_{u,t\rightarrow t+1}$, is a cross-period statistic on the
distribution of technical categories and pairs, assessed separately with its
own null. Keeping $T$ outside the concurrent profile prevents a circular
reading in which future structural change is used to describe a state and the
state is then said to explain that change; the five dimensions together still
characterize the capability, four synchronously and one longitudinally.
\input{figures/figure1}

Two temporal rules follow. First, each statistic is dated by the latest
information required to determine it, not by the earliest observable signal:
$A$ and $R$ are available when an invention is published, $E$ becomes
available only when its cohort closes (it uses the cohort's subclass-growth
shares), and $D$ requires a fixed five-year follow-up so that its
cohort-relative ranks can mature. The profile $\mathbf{x}_{ut}$ is therefore a
retrospective portrait of a closed cohort, not a contemporaneously observable
state at the cohort date, and no indicator uses information published after its
own observation window. Second, a profile at one period is a state, not a
trajectory; trajectory statements require ordered profiles for the same
institution and evidence that observed change exceeds a within-university null.

Three interpretive development patterns are retained as \emph{hypotheses to be
checked against these observables}, not as classes to be imposed. A
\emph{steady-utilization} pattern shows a stable technical core: low $T$, low
$E$, and moderate-to-high $D$ on an established knowledge base. An
\emph{exploratory-innovation} pattern shows high $E$ and/or high $R$, that is,
the university repeatedly enters novel technological directions and forms
unusual combinations. A \emph{structural-recombination} pattern requires $T$
above its null in at least one transition and persistence of the new
composition in the following period, indicating a sustained reorganization of
the portfolio. An institution may follow different patterns at different times,
mixed cases remain visible, and the patterns form no performance ranking.

\section{Data and observable relations}\label{sec:data}
\subsection{Corpus and its boundaries}
The source is the research team's database of Chinese university invention
patents and their citation records, covering 124 Double-First-Class
universities over 1990--2019. Table~\ref{tab:data} lists the coverage items
that must be audited before analysis. Its rows determine which relations in
the framework are observable and are therefore part of the design rather than
a formality.
\input{tables/table1}

The primary cohort comprises published Chinese invention applications with at
least one university applicant at first publication, restricted to the 124
Double-First-Class universities over 1990--2019, regardless of later grant
status; utility models and designs are excluded. Institutional coverage is
defined by a dated roster of the 124 Double-First-Class universities, with
historical names, mergers, and affiliated hospitals resolved through
time-specific linkage; ambiguous matches remain auditable rather than silently
assigned. Publication and grant versions
of an application are collapsed. If family identifiers are available, the
invention unit is a simple family represented by its earliest eligible
document; otherwise it is the deduplicated application, and the unit is
declared. A patent with $m_p$ university applicants contributes
$\omega_{up}=1/m_p$ to each; full counts are a sensitivity.

Three properties of a university-only corpus constrain the design and are
stated here rather than discovered later. (i)~Backward references from
university patents are observable, but the cited documents' own attributes
are available only when the cited record is itself in the corpus or its
classification is carried in the reference field. (ii)~Forward citations to
university patents are observable; the citing documents' applicants are
observable only where the citation record carries applicant fields, and the
citing documents' \emph{own} forward links are generally not available. Reuse
depth beyond one citation step is therefore excluded from the primary measure.
(iii)~Chinese invention patents receive few forward citations in their first
year, so any indicator conditioned on early recognition will be zero for most
records. Emergence is consequently measured in an early-information form that
does not depend on recognition, with a recognized variant as a secondary
measure.

\subsection{Observation time}
First publication is the main clock. $W_t=[b_t,b_{t+1})$ is a non-overlapping
three-year cohort and $B_t=[b_t-3,b_t)$ its historical reference; reuse uses a
horizon of $H=5$ years. Mature profiles require $b_{t+1}+H\leq d_{\mathrm{extract}}$,
and cohorts without a full historical reference are excluded. The number of
mature cohorts this leaves is a property of the database, to be reported in
Table~\ref{tab:data}; if fewer than three consecutive mature cohorts exist,
trajectory analysis is restricted to description and the transformation test
is reported as infeasible rather than approximated.

A university-period enters profile analysis with at least 20 attributed
inventions and at least 80\% usable attributed mass for the fields each
dimension requires. Confirmed absence of citations is zero; an unavailable
field is missing and never enters a denominator. For dimension $j$ the eligible
subset is $P^j_{ut}$ with mass $M^j_{ut}=\sum_{p\in P^j_{ut}}\omega_{up}$,
reported as a share of total mass.

\subsection{Three relation layers}
Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows how the corpus is decomposed. The technology
layer has IPC main-group nodes linked by fractional co-occurrence,
\begin{equation}
w_{ij}^{(t)}=\sum_{p\in W_t:\,m_p^C\geq2}
\frac{\ind\{i,j\in\mathcal{C}_p\}}{\binom{m_p^C}{2}},\quad i<j,
\label{eq:cooc}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{C}_p$ is the set of distinct main groups on patent $p$; each
multi-coded invention contributes one unit of pair mass. The semantic layer
has patent nodes linked by cosine similarity of character 2--4-gram TF--IDF
vectors of normalized titles and abstracts, with vocabulary and IDF fitted on
$B_t$ only (features in at least five and at most 80\% of reference documents,
capped at 100{,}000; log TF, smoothed IDF, $L_2$ normalization). The citation
layer has patent nodes with directed edges from cited to citing document,
duplicate family pairs collapsed, within-family links removed, and citing
publication required to be strictly later. Applicant-overlap self-citations are
excluded from the primary reuse graph. A patent--IPC incidence relation
connects the layers; their adjacency matrices are never summed. Display graphs
(normalized edges, top-10 neighbor union; mutual 15-nearest-neighbor semantic
graph; Leiden communities, \citealp{traag2019}) use fixed coordinates across
periods; all indicators use unpruned weights.

\section{Measures}\label{sec:measures}
\subsection{Knowledge sourcing}
Let $q_{ut}$ be the attributed share of valid inventions with at least one
resolved external backward reference, and let $\pi_{ut,k}$ be the fractional
distribution of those references over a fixed, version-harmonized IPC subclass
vocabulary of size $K_t$. Then
\begin{equation}
A_{ut}=q_{ut}\left[\tfrac12+\tfrac12\,
\frac{-\sum_k\pi_{ut,k}\log\pi_{ut,k}}{\log K_t}\right],
\label{eq:acquisition}
\end{equation}
so that confirmed absence of external sourcing gives $A=0$ and the positive
baseline distinguishes narrow sourcing from none. Both components are
reported. $A$ measures the reach and diversity of documented knowledge
sourcing \citep{shannon1948,stirling2007}, not absorptive capacity
\citep{cohen1990}. The $\tfrac12$ positive baseline is a reporting convention
that keeps the no-sourcing and narrow-sourcing cases separable rather than a
substantive equivalence between them; both components ($q_{ut}$ and the
diversity term) are reported so the weighting is auditable. When $K_t\leq1$ or
no classifiable external reference exists, the diversity term is undefined and
the period is treated as missing rather than zero.

\subsection{Recombination}
Let $c^-_{ij,t}$ be the historical fractional pair mass of main groups
$(i,j)$ in $B_t$, $Q_t$ the number of possible pairs in the pre-established
vocabulary, and $C_t=\sum_{i<j}c^-_{ij,t}>0$. With $\alpha=1/2$,
\begin{align}
p^-_{ij,t}&=\frac{c^-_{ij,t}+\alpha}{C_t+\alpha Q_t},&
r_{ij,t}&=\frac{-\log p^-_{ij,t}}{-\log\{\alpha/(C_t+\alpha Q_t)\}},\\
R_{ut}&=\frac{1}{M^R_{ut}}\sum_{p\in P^R_{ut}}\omega_{up}\,
\frac{\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{L}_p}r_{ij,t}}{|\mathcal{L}_p|},
\label{eq:recombination}
\end{align}
where $\mathcal{L}_p$ is the set of valid mapped pairs on patent $p$.
Unresolved codes map to one recorded out-of-vocabulary category; a patent with
no valid pair is missing, not zero. Rarity indexes unusual combination, not
novelty or usefulness \citep{fleming2001,youn2015,verhoeven2016}. Because a pair can be rare merely because its components are individually
uncommon, $R$ is interpreted conditional on a degree-preserving permutation
null that holds marginal code frequencies; the observed-minus-permuted rarity
is reported alongside $R$, and the share of single-coded patents (necessarily
excluded from pairing) is reported as a coverage indicator. First-seen pair
rates provide an additional check.

\subsection{Technological exploration}
Semantic novelty is $n_{p,t}=1-\max_{q\in B_t}s_{pq}$ over the full historical
index, excluding same-family comparators. For subclass $k$, with fractional
shares $\rho_{k,t}$ in the current cohort and $\rho^-_{k,t}$ in the reference,
\begin{equation}
g_{k,t}=\max\!\left\{0,\frac{\rho_{k,t}-\rho^-_{k,t}}
{\rho_{k,t}+\rho^-_{k,t}+10^{-12}}\right\},\qquad
E_{ut}=\frac{1}{M^E_{ut}}\sum_{p\in P^E_{ut}}\omega_{up}\,n_{p,t}\,g_{p,t},
\label{eq:emergence}
\end{equation}
where $g_{p,t}$ averages over the patent's subclasses. Both $n_{p,t}$ and
$g_{k,t}$ are computed against the corpus-wide historical index $B_t$ and
current cohort $W_t$ (field-level signals), not against a university's own
past; ``technological exploration'' is therefore read as the university
producing work that is novel relative to the field's history \emph{and} located
in growing subclasses, not as institutional unfamiliarity per se. Because
$g_{p,t}$ uses the current cohort's subclass shares, $E$ becomes available
only when the cohort closes; it is early-information in that it needs no
citation or recognition, but it is retrospective at the cohort level rather
than observable at a single patent's publication. The novelty--growth product
also weights exploration toward expanding directions, so novelty in stable or
declining subclasses receives no growth credit; the recognized variant and
case reading compensate. A recognized variant $E^{\mathrm{rec}}$ multiplies
each product by an indicator of external citation within one year; given
constraint~(iii) in Section~\ref{sec:data}, it is reported with its nonzero
share as a secondary measure and is not the primary exploration indicator.
Neither variant covers the full construct of technological emergence
\citep{rotolo2015,small2014}; technical coherence is assessed through cases.

\subsection{Knowledge diffusion}
Within $(\tau_p,\tau_p+H]$, let $b_p$ count distinct external citing
organizations where applicant fields are available and distinct external
citing families otherwise (the level is recorded), and let
$v_p=1-\Delta_p/H$ when the first external citation arrives after $\Delta_p$
years, with $v_p=0$ for uncited patents. Each component is transformed to its
midrank among positive values in the same primary subclass and cohort
($\phi(0)=0$; back-off to IPC section and then cohort below 30 positives), and
\begin{equation}
D_{ut}=\frac{1}{2M^D_{ut}}\sum_{p\in P^D_{ut}}\omega_{up}
\left[\phi^b(b_p)+\phi^v(v_p)\right].
\label{eq:diffusion}
\end{equation}
$D$ measures \emph{documented knowledge diffusion among observed citers}.
Midranks are rescaled to $[0,1]$ so the two components share a common scale;
where applicant fields are missing, externality cannot be established and such
records are flagged and excluded from $b_p$ rather than silently classified as
external or treated as confirmed zeros. Because the citing side of a
university-only corpus is incomplete, $D$ does not
measure economy-wide diffusion, and a two-step depth component is not part of
the primary measure; it is computed as a sensitivity only where descendants'
forward links exist. First uptake and accumulated breadth carry different
information \citep{huang2019} and are also reported separately.

\subsection{Dynamic transformation}
Let $\mathbf{a}_{ut}$ and $\mathbf{r}_{ut}$ be the university's normalized
subclass and pair distributions on shared vocabularies. Then
\begin{equation}
T_{u,t\rightarrow t+1}=\frac{
\JSD(\mathbf{a}_{ut},\mathbf{a}_{u,t+1})+
\JSD(\mathbf{r}_{ut},\mathbf{r}_{u,t+1})}{2\log2}\in[0,1],
\label{eq:transformation}
\end{equation}
with $\JSD$ the Jensen--Shannon divergence \citep{lin1991}. A transformation
event requires $T$ above the 95th percentile of a within-university null that
pools the two adjacent cohorts and permutes their period labels while
preserving cohort sizes, confirmed under size-matched subsampling. A
\emph{sustained} event additionally requires the third period to be closer to
the new composition than to the old and still distinguishable from the old.
Mergers and IPC revision events are audited before any event is retained. The
null exchanges patents between the two adjacent periods conditional on the
pooled portfolio and cohort sizes; it therefore tests period-label
exchangeability, not the magnitude of year-to-year variation under a
temporally structured process, and its validity depends on the absence of
secular trends, coverage change, or field-wide transitions during the window.
Because pair changes partly follow subclass-composition changes, the two JSD
terms are reported separately as well as summed, so the incremental
contribution of the pair term is visible; multiple university-transition tests
are controlled at the family-wise level. $T$ measures institutional
reorganization of a portfolio, not a technological paradigm shift
\citep{dosi1982,funk2017}; a large $T$ may reflect disruption or contraction as
readily as adaptive change, and diversity is a distinct property
\citep{stirling2007,porter2009}.

\section{Analysis and reporting design}\label{sec:analysis}
The analysis proceeds in five steps, each answerable from the corpus described
above.

\emph{Landscape.} The technology layer is laid out once on the pooled corpus
(Figure~\ref{fig:map}A); period overlays reuse the same coordinates
(Figure~\ref{fig:map}B) so that entry, persistence, and exit of technical
groups reflect the data rather than the layout. Coverage growth is evaluated
against rarefied subclass coverage and portfolio size, since a larger sample
alone makes more fields visible. Disagreement between the semantic and
classification views marks cases for inspection.

\emph{Profiles.} Distributions, zero shares, and denominators of $A,R,E,D$ are
reported for eligible university-periods (Table~\ref{tab:descriptive}), together
with pairwise correlations and their association with log patent volume, field
composition, and period. Residual variation after these adjustments is the
evidence that relational profiles add information beyond size and field.
Profiles are then displayed on their original scales with whole-university
bootstrap intervals \citep{efron1979}, and universities are placed in profile
space (Figure~\ref{fig:states}A).

\emph{Patterns.} Development patterns are identified in two ways and then
compared. First, the three a-priori patterns of Section~\ref{sec:framework}
(steady utilization, exploratory innovation, structural recombination) are
applied through their stated observable criteria using thresholds prespecified
before inspection, and the counts of supported, mixed, and unsupported cases
are reported. Second, a Gaussian mixture on the concurrent profile
$(A,R,E,D)$, with the number of components selected by BIC and cluster-wise
stability \citep{fraley2002,hennig2007}, classifies university-period
profiles into GMM states. Because the a-priori patterns are trajectory-level
(multi-period) while GMM states are period-level, the cross-check first
derives a trajectory label per university from its GMM-state sequence under the
same prespecified rules, then compares the two labelings by adjusted Rand
index on the common unit (universities) (Table~\ref{tab:gmm}); GMM adequacy
is assessed for bounded and zero-inflated variables, skewed masses, and
repeated observations from the same university. For universities with
consecutive eligible cohorts, a first-order Markov transition matrix among
the derived trajectory labels reports how universities move between labels
over time (Table~\ref{tab:markov}), with classification uncertainty and the
selective availability of consecutive cohorts reflected in the estimates.
Ordered profiles are displayed alongside $T$ and its
null (Figure~\ref{fig:states}B). The proposition that sustained exploration or
recombination precedes structural transformation is tested by comparing,
within each university, the observed frequency of a ``high-$R$/$E$ period
followed by a sustained $T$ event'' with its frequency under order
permutations of that university's own profile values, recomputing $T$ events
from the reordered sequence. The contrast is reported with a permutation
interval; a nonpositive or unstable contrast leaves the proposition
unsupported. The test assumes the profile values are exchangeable in time
within a university, which is violated under strong trends or serial
dependence and is checked via the sensitivity to window length.

\emph{Scale versus structure.} To describe whether the capability structure
differs beyond output scale, universities are grouped into patent-volume
strata and the within-stratum heterogeneity of the five-dimension profile is
compared against a measurement-noise null estimated from the within-university
bootstrap of each profile, rather than against zero; this separates genuine
institutional differences from sampling and measurement error. The share of
profile variance remaining after regression on log volume, field composition,
and period is reported as descriptive evidence of variation not explained by
scale or field, not as validation of the measures. The five-dimension
capability structure is then compared with traditional output
indicators---patent count, grant count, and forward-citation frequency (and
incoPat quality ratings where available)---by partial correlation after
volume control and by Kendall's $\tau$ between the two rank orderings of
universities; because no single capability score is asserted, the ranking
uses a prespecified scalar composite (e.g., the first principal component of
the standardized profile) reported with its uncertainty. Low partial
correlation and divergent rankings are read as descriptive evidence that the
two families capture different things, not as proof that one is the more
valid measure.

\emph{Cases and sensitivity.} Twelve trajectories sampled across profile
regions and size strata are assessed by two technically qualified raters from
dated patent evidence without model values; agreement, disagreements, and
adjudication are reported \citep{cohen1960}. Sensitivity covers invention
unit, attribution, text representation (TF--IDF versus a frozen
Chinese-capable encoder, \citealp{reimers2019}), window length, citation
horizon and self-citation scope, eligibility thresholds, and the $E$ and $D$
variants (Table~\ref{tab:robustness}). Common-sample agreement is reported
separately from sample retention. All secondary associations are exploratory.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
The framework's contribution to information science is both representational
and empirical. Each inference is tied to the relation that supports it: an
unusual IPC pair supports a combination claim; semantic distance from a dated
reference corpus supports a technological-exploration claim; a later external
citing record supports a knowledge-diffusion claim; divergence between
successive category distributions, against a within-university null, supports
a dynamic-transformation claim. Retaining these origins makes agreement and
disagreement among representations visible and connects patent mapping
\citep{kay2014,leydesdorff2014,yan2017} to capability measurement without
collapsing the relations into one score.

Empirically, the analysis examines whether universities with similar patent
output differ in their five-dimension capability structures and whether the
three development patterns mark distinct paths: steady-utilization
universities consolidate and diffuse an established core;
exploratory-innovation universities repeatedly enter novel directions and
form unusual combinations; structural-recombination universities reorganize
their portfolios across periods. These paths are not a performance ranking;
they describe how portfolios differ, not the causes of advantage formation,
and the observational design supports differences and temporal associations
rather than mechanisms.

The design also states what a university-only corpus can support. It can
describe knowledge sourcing, recombination, technological exploration, and
knowledge diffusion among observed citers, and it can test whether a
portfolio's composition changed more than expected under period-label
exchangeability within the university. It cannot establish economy-wide
diffusion, reuse chains beyond one step, or the
causes of change.

Other limits persist. Patents omit scientific, tacit, and unpatented knowledge.
Citations do not observe learning or contractual transfer \citep{alcacer2006}.
Novelty is relative to a corpus and a representation. Institutional events
such as mergers, recruitment, and policy affect both profiles and later change.
Profiles should therefore be read with their coverage and uncertainty,
consistent with responsible use of research metrics \citep{hicks2015}, and not
as a ranking.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
This paper connects the contents of patent records, their dated relations, and
the time at which each relation becomes observable into a framework for
measuring university patent technological innovation capability from patent
knowledge-association structures. Four concurrent dimensions---knowledge
sourcing, recombination, technological exploration, and knowledge
diffusion---are measured from separate relation layers; dynamic transformation
is assessed independently as sustained compositional change. Applied to 124
Double-First-Class universities over 1990--2019, the framework is used to examine whether
similar-output universities differ in capability structure and whether they
fall into three development patterns---steady utilization, exploratory
innovation, and structural recombination---offering a process-based lens on
how innovation capability is described that stops short of causal claims
about its formation. The framework is specified against the actual boundaries of
a university-only patent and citation corpus, so that its empirical claims are
limited to what the data observe. Extensions with enterprise citing records
would allow diffusion to be traced beyond the academic system, and
publication--patent links would separate scientific from technological
sourcing.
